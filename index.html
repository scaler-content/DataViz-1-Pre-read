1What's Happening in This Lecture

Here's a sneak peek at everything we cover today, and why each one is worth showing up for.

TopicWhat It Covers
Exploratory vs ExplanatoryYou already know how to make a chart. Today starts with the question of what the chart is for. Exploratory charts are many, quick and for you, to find something. Explanatory charts are one, deliberate and for someone else (a board, a client, a manager), to communicate a single finding. Every other skill in this lecture turns the first kind into the second.
Matplotlib Architecture: Figure, Axes, AxisSeaborn draws the chart; Matplotlib owns the canvas it's drawn on. You'll learn the three objects every chart is made of: the Figure (the canvas), the Axes (one plot on it) and the Axis (an x or y ruler). You'll also learn the two ways to drive them: the quick plt. style you've used so far, and the fig, ax = plt.subplots() object API that gives you control over every chart.
Styling & TitlesYou'll take a plain bar chart and turn it, step by step, into one a board reads in seconds. That means colour used with intent (one highlight, everything else grey), removing clutter, and a title that states the finding. You'll also control legends, gridlines and ticks for charts with more than one series, all through ax. methods.
Subplots & the Mini DashboardA dashboard is one Figure holding several Axes that tell one connected story. You'll use plt.subplots(rows, cols) to get a grid of plots, index them like a table, and compose a 2 × 2 one-page summary. Each panel is titled with its finding, and fig.suptitle() and fig.tight_layout() finish the page.
Chart Selection FrameworkYou'll pick the chart from the question, not the question from the chart. Two things decide it: the shape of the data (how many variables, categorical or continuous) and the job (explore or explain). This framework ties together every chart you've learned so far and previews where multivariate charts fit next.
Stacked & Dodged BarsWhen both variables are categories, a single bar per group hides what's inside it. Stacked bars show each group's total and its composition; dodged (side-by-side) bars make one segment easy to compare across groups. You'll build both and learn which question each one answers.
Using AI WellAI tools such as Gemini in Colab are fast at boilerplate: subplot grids, tick formatting, rewriting plt. code as ax. code, explaining errors. You'll draw the line on what stays yours: choosing the chart, finding the insight, deciding what to highlight, and writing the title.

2Real-World Use Cases

  • Exploratory vs Explanatory: A product analyst makes a dozen quick charts to explore churn by plan, then rebuilds only the one that shows the problem for the leadership review.
  • Matplotlib Architecture: Figure, Axes, Axis: An analyst inherits an old notebook full of plt. calls that set titles on the wrong chart, and rewrites it with fig, ax so every title, label and limit lands on the intended plot.
  • Styling & Titles: A category manager's slide shows revenue by category in grey with only the fastest-growing category in colour, under the title "Home Décor grew fastest this quarter". The room gets the point before anyone speaks.
  • Subplots & the Mini Dashboard: A growth analyst puts sign-ups, activation rate, revenue and churn on one 2 × 2 page for the weekly business review, each panel titled with its takeaway.
  • Chart Selection Framework: Asked "how spread out are delivery times?", an operations analyst reaches for a histogram rather than a bar chart, because the question is about one continuous variable.
  • Stacked & Dodged Bars: A marketing analyst uses a stacked bar to show each city's orders split by payment method, then a dodged bar to compare UPI usage across cities.
  • Using AI Well: An analyst asks Gemini to scaffold a 2 × 2 subplot grid in seconds, then spends their own time choosing what each panel shows and writing the one-line titles.
